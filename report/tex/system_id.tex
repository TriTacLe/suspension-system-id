\subsubsection*{Question 1: what is the gain of the system?}

For a step of size $U$, the final value theorem applied to Equation~\ref{eqn:canonical} gives

\begin{equation}
    x_\infty = \lim_{s\to0} s\,G(s)\frac{U}{s} = G(0)\,U = A\,U,
\end{equation}

so the gain is the change in displacement divided by the size of the step,

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:gain}
    A = \frac{x_\infty - x_0}{U} = \frac{\gap{final} - \gap{baseline}}{\gap{U}} = \gap{A}~\text{m/V}.
\end{equation}

The baseline $x_0$ is subtracted because the suspension rests at a non-zero displacement
before the step, visible on the left of Figure~\ref{fig:step}. The input value used is the
one recorded in the log rather than the number typed into the simulator, in case the
simulator scales it. From Equation~\ref{eqn:identities}, $k = 1/A = \gap{k}$.

\subsubsection*{Question 2: does the system have real or complex poles?}

The response in Figure~\ref{fig:step} \gap{oscillates about its final value / rises to it
without overshoot}, so the system is \gap{underdamped, $\zeta < 1$ / overdamped,
$\zeta > 1$} and the poles are therefore \gap{a complex conjugate pair / two distinct real
poles}. Oscillation about the final value is only possible when the poles have a non-zero
imaginary part, which is what makes this readable straight off the shape of the curve.

\subsubsection*{Question 3: where are the dominant poles?}

For an underdamped second order system the fractional overshoot $M_p$ measured from
Figure~\ref{fig:step} fixes the damping ratio,

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:zeta}
    M_p = e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \zeta = \frac{-\ln M_p}{\sqrt{\pi^2 + \ln^2 M_p}} = \gap{zeta},
\end{equation}

and the spacing of successive peaks fixes the damped natural frequency,

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:wd}
    \omega_d = \frac{2\pi}{T_d} = \frac{2\pi}{\gap{Td}} = \gap{wd}~\text{rad/s},
    \qquad
    \omega_n = \frac{\omega_d}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} = \gap{wn}~\text{rad/s}.
\end{equation}

The poles are then

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:poles}
    s_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_n \pm j\omega_d = \gap{sigma} \pm j\,\gap{wd}.
\end{equation}

Averaging the period over every peak in the record rather than using one pair reduces the
effect of the 100~ms sampling, and the damping ratio is cross-checked by the logarithmic
decrement over the same peaks, which gives \gap{zeta from log decrement} against
\gap{zeta from overshoot} from Equation~\ref{eqn:zeta}. \gap{Comment on how close the two
readings are and which is used going forward.}

\subsubsection*{Question 4: what corner frequency follows from that?}

For a second order system with no zeros the magnitude asymptotes cross at the undamped
natural frequency, so the expected corner frequency is $\omega_n = \gap{wn}$~rad/s. This is
a prediction made from the step data alone, and it is what the frequency response test in
Section~\ref{sec:freq} is used to confirm.

\subsection{Frequency Response}\label{sec:freq}

The simulator applies one sinusoid at a time rather than sweeping, so the frequencies have
to be chosen in advance. They are placed around the corner frequency predicted in
Question~4, since that is where the magnitude peak and the $-90^\circ$ phase crossing sit
and where the curve carries the most information about $\zeta$. Two points well below and
two well above $\omega_n$ fix the low frequency gain and the high frequency roll-off, which
is what confirms the system is second order rather than first.

Substituting Table~\ref{tab:model} into Equation~\ref{eqn:tf}, the identified plant is

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:identified}
    G(s) = \frac{1}{s^2 + \gap{b}\,s + \gap{k}}.
\end{equation}

The two coefficients come from different measurements, so they fail differently: $k$ rests
on the final value, which is the most reliable number in the record, while $b$ rests on the
overshoot, which the coarse sampling biases. \gap{State which parameter you trust more and
why, given your own data.}
