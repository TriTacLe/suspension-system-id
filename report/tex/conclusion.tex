\section{Conclusion}

The suspension keyed to student number LXXTRI004 was identified as

\begin{equation}
    G(s) = \frac{0.921\,e^{-0.101s}}{s^2 + 1.310\,s + 1.150},
\end{equation}

an underdamped second order system with gain $0.801$ m/V, damping ratio $0.611$ and natural
frequency $1.072$ rad/s, giving a spring coefficient of $1.150$, a damping coefficient of
$1.310$ and poles at $-0.655 \pm j\,0.849$. The input is scaled by $\alpha = 0.921$ and not
applied as a force directly, and the measurement chain adds a delay of one sample period.

The step test supplied all three parameters and the two experiments agree. Fitting gain,
damping ratio, natural frequency and delay to the twelve frequency points, using none of the
step record, confirms the damping ratio to within $0.1$\% and the natural frequency to within
$0.5$\%. That agreement needs the delay term. Read straight off the plot, the phase crosses
$-90^\circ$ at $1.003$~rad/s rather than at $\omega_n$, which is the delay showing itself
before it is fitted.

The main limitation is the $0.001$~m amplitude resolution of the logger. It leaves the first
overshoot and the undershoot that follows it measurable, at $71$ and $6$ resolution steps, but
puts the second overshoot at half a step, visible but too coarse to measure, so the time domain gives two
readings of the damping ratio where a finer logger would give several. The $100$~ms sample
period costs far less than that, $0.1$~mm at worst against a damped period of $7.4$~s, though
it is the source of the delay term above. The one thing left unresolved is the size of that
delay. The frequency points put it at $101$~ms and the step record at $58$~ms, and the repeats
rule out the easy explanation, since three runs triggered at different times all return $58$~ms
with no scatter. The zero order hold on the sampled input would account for the difference,
contributing $50$~ms of phase to a sine sweep and nothing to a single step edge, but it also
predicts an attenuation the measured magnitude does not show, so it is a hypothesis and not a
finding. Nothing in the data calls for a third pole. The measured roll-off is $-39.1$~dB per
decade over the five points above $2\omega_n$ against $-40$ for two poles, so whatever faster
dynamics the suspension has sit above the $10$~rad/s the sweep reached and never became
visible.
